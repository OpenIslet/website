Exactly three years ago today, I was in hospital. My one-year-old daughter had just been diagnosed with type 1 diabetes (T1D). She was clinging to me, and I could feel her willing me to save her — but I couldn’t. I’ve never felt so helpless in my life. In that moment, I made a promise to myself: that one day, I would tell her she is cured.

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks and destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. It affects nearly 10 million people worldwide, and over the last decade cases have been rising relentlessly. Diagnosis is a traumatic process, and hundreds of thousands of families go through it every year. The worst of it is that not every child makes it through — which is why I consider us lucky. Those who do are left with the ongoing burden of juggling unpredictable, 24/7 blood sugar management.

A hundred years ago, T1D was a death sentence — until Frederick Banting and his colleagues discovered and developed insulin, then sold the patent rights for $1 and saved the lives of millions. Outcomes have slowly improved ever since, and more recently we’ve seen leaps forward with new technologies such as CGMs and insulin pumps, which now work together as automated insulin delivery (AID) systems. Yet despite all of this, managing diabetes is still threaded through every single part of life for those living with T1D — and for those caring for someone who has it.

T1D is not new to me. My mum has it too, and ever since I was a child I remember reading about a “cure” on the horizon. But the horizon always seems to stay on the horizon. Biotech has moved faster over the last decade, and I’ve felt the potential for a cure get a little closer, but it still seems a long way away, and not coming fast enough.

Over the last year, I’ve watched the pace of change with AI accelerate. And about six months ago, I realised we were close to a point where frontier AI models would put the power of some of the greatest scientific minds in the world into the hands of everyone — a moment when frontier models would have the capability to make connections we cannot currently see to drive change faster. Today is that day. Around a month ago AI models reached the intelligence level where anyone can now run the equivalent of thousands of the brightest minds in the world, all at once, and pit them against each other to solve some of the biggest problems that face humanity. At the top of my list is helping to find a cure for T1D.

I’m sure there are others pursuing this behind closed doors, but I don’t see it happening out in the open yet — and I think we can move faster. So today I’m launching OpenIslet: a global open-source project to rapidly develop a blueprint for curing type 1 diabetes, harnessing AI to design a personalised cure that aims to work without anti-rejection drugs. And we are doing it together, out in the open. This idea partly builds on the back of the T1D community itself — a large global group that is open and inclusive, but at the same time tight-knit and personal. A community of people who help each other and drive change — renowned for their resilience and ingenuity, with the “we are not waiting” movement already spearheading dozens of life-changing projects. T1D does not discriminate by location, gender, ethnicity, or wealth — it impacts everyone: sport stars, CEOs, doctors, software engineers, Prime Ministers. That diversity brings a breadth and depth of knowledge and wisdom that is unique.

To jump-start OpenIslet I have developed and stress-tested three candidate blueprints across five layers, to design a personalised, allogeneic, hypoimmunogenic islet cell to cure type 1 diabetes.

First, across the published evidence: adversarial swarms of frontier AI agents from leading AI labs were pitted against each other across 563 studies — identifying the exact reasons current “cures” fail, and architecting novel solutions to form a candidate blueprint that was then attacked from every angle, around the clock for 3 weeks.

Second, at the DNA level: single-letter genetic edits were scored with AlphaGenome, and two of the cell’s protective genes were wired into the cell’s own natural inflammation alarm.

Third, at the protein level: AlphaFold models and 3D crystal structures of the natural proteins were checked on paper against the design, to see whether the protective proteins should fit their targets on immune cells and tell them not to attack.

Fourth, at the tissue level: the first 14 days after transplant were simulated inside an ultra-thin protective pouch — testing how oxygen flows into the cells, with a separate 24-hour model of blood sugar and insulin after meals, to solve the early oxygen starvation that kills most transplanted cells.

And fifth, across thousands of virtual patients: because no two people have the same immune system, the blueprint was run across 10,000 simulated patients from diverse backgrounds around the world — tailoring the genetic edits for people whose immune systems would reject a standard, off-the-shelf cell.

Together, these five layers make up the first blueprint: Islet-1.

There are still weaknesses in the blueprint, but they are now estimated to be less likely to cause an issue than not, and any further fix we thought of would have hurt another part of the design. However, I know this still isn’t the best we can do — I’m certain it isn’t. So I’m opening it up to everyone: please attack it, find the holes in it, suggest improvements, or propose a completely new blueprint altogether. And the pipeline does not stop at Islet-1. Because AI capabilities are increasing at an exponential pace, the aim is to ride that frontier, recursively improving and evolving the blueprint every three months. Each release is a new version — Islet-1.1, Islet-1.2, and on — and that does not stop until type 1 diabetes is cured.

And while simulating a cure on a computer is not the same as curing a person in the real world (only real experiments in a wet lab can prove what works), what AI tools do is let us test and rule out well over a thousand flawed designs first. This means that when scientists step up to the lab bench, they start from the strongest possible position. Anyone can head to OpenIslet.org, run the current blueprint through their own AI chat or agentic harness in a few simple steps, and submit their findings as an Islet Improvement Proposal (IIP) to be recognised as an OpenIslet contributor.

You also don’t have to go through OpenIslet at all. Anyone can take the blueprint, or any part of it, and run with it. You don’t need permission to build on it, adapt it, or change it however you like. The goal is to cure type 1 diabetes. The winning recipe is simply the one that delivers a safe and durable cure, and it doesn’t matter how we get there or who gets there first.

There are AI models and scientific minds out there far more capable than the ones I have access to, and this is a call to action for all of them. To the scientists: tell me why this won’t work, and tell me how we can make it better. To the AI labs: point your most powerful unreleased models at this problem, think inside and outside the box, and let’s start benchmarking frontier AI against real change. And to everyone reading this who has T1D, cares for someone with T1D, or knows someone with T1D: do everything you can to spread this message like wildfire and get it in front of the people who can turn this blueprint into reality.

Let’s ride the frontier together, right through to the moment we can hear the words: “You are cured.”
